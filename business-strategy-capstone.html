Project Overview

From rocky start to above-average finish

Capsim places teams in charge of a simulated manufacturing company, with decisions each round across pricing, production, R&D, HR, and finance. Every choice ripples across the whole business — which is exactly what our team underestimated early on.

The strategy

We started with a pure low-cost strategy: keep prices low, invest in automation, and win on cost. It didn't hold up — cutting prices too far hurt margins, and ignoring high-end, performance, and size segments capped our growth. We shifted to a broad low-cost strategy: stay efficient, but compete across every segment instead of writing off the ones that didn't fit a narrow cost story.

The bigger lesson wasn't about pricing at all — it was about forecasting discipline and treating the company as one connected system instead of five separate departments.

Final outcome

Ferris wasn't the top-performing company in the simulation, but by Round 8 we were outperforming the industry average in both revenue and profit — a direct result of correcting our forecasting and treating decisions as interconnected rather than siloed.

Performance

A rough start, then a steady climb

Revenue and earnings tell the same story: two difficult early rounds driven by forecasting mistakes, followed by a recovery that outpaced the industry average.

Revenue over time

Ferris trailed the industry average through Round 2, then pulled ahead and stayed above it through Round 8 — though Garrett grew faster and finished as the market leader.

Earnings over time

After a $4.0M loss in Round 2, profit grew faster than revenue every round after — a sign of improving efficiency, not just scale.

Profit grew faster than sales

From Round 3 onward, earnings compounded faster than revenue — evidence that the automation investment and tighter forecasting were making the company more efficient, not just bigger.

What Went Wrong (and Right)

Two rounds, two opposite mistakes

Rounds 1 and 2 taught the same lesson from opposite directions — and automation became the decision that changed our trajectory.

Round 1

Overforecasted demand

$115.6M in sales and a thin $1.0M profit — but we overspent and overproduced, triggering a $12.1M emergency loan that hurt our stock price.

Round 2

Overcorrected into stockouts

Trying to avoid excess inventory, we swung too conservative and stocked out on several products. Sales dropped to $112.8M and we lost $4.0M.

Turning point

Automation paid off

Painful early on cash flow, but it matched our low-cost strategy. Contribution margin climbed from 32.2% in Round 1 to 52.8% by Round 8.

Round 3 onward

Started thinking as one system

We stopped treating marketing, production, finance, HR, and R&D as separate decisions and started checking how each choice affected the others.

Process Improvements

What we'd do differently next time

The clearest gap wasn't strategy — it was process. We didn't stress-test decisions against the rest of the company before locking them in.

Pre-submission checklist

Check cash balance, inventory risk, production cushion, and contribution margin before locking in every round's decisions.

Assign a forecast challenger

One person's job: poke holes in the demand forecast before it's finalized, instead of accepting the first estimate.

Assign a financing challenger

A second person stress-tests the financing plan against worst-case demand swings to catch emergency-loan risk early.

Treat departments as connected

Ask whether a production call could cause a stockout, whether automation leaves enough cash, and whether a price cut is worth the margin loss — every round.

Avoid overreacting

Round 2's stockouts came from overcorrecting Round 1's overproduction. Smaller, steadier adjustments beat swinging between extremes.

Revisit segment coverage

A pure low-cost focus capped growth. Staying competitive across high-end, performance, and size segments broadened the path to recovery.
